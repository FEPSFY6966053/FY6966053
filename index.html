Abstract

Coastal flooding is an increasing challenge that threatens communities, infrastructure and ecosystems worldwide. Traditional coastal protection methods such as seawalls and breakwaters provide effective defence against flooding but often require high initial construction costs and long-term maintenance. As a result, nature-based solutions are heavily considered within civil engineering. Mangrove forests act as natural wave attenuators by reducing wave energy before it reaches the shoreline through their dense root systems, trunks and vegetation. In addition to reducing wave height, mangroves stabilise sediment and minimise coastal erosion, contributing to the long-term resilience of coastal environments.This article evaluates the mechanisms through which mangroves reduce wave energy and assesses their advantages, limitations and potential hybrid applications. The study highlights how mangroves can be integrated with conventional coastal defences to create hybrid systems that improve flood resilience whilst also maintaining sustainability.

Introduction

Coastal flooding is an ongoing challenge for engineers, especially in Southeast Asia where storm surges, coastal erosion and rising sea levels threaten communities and infrastructure. As pressure on existing flood defence systems increases, there is growing interest in solutions that not only provide protection but also work with natural coastal processes. Among these approaches, mangrove forests have emerged as a strong nature-based defence due to their ability to influence wave behaviour before it reaches the shoreline. Rather than resisting wave forces, mangroves reduce wave energy through their complex root systems and vegetation structure. This article evaluates how mangroves function as natural wave attenuators and examines their potential role within modern coastal flood defence systems.

Mechanisms of Wave Attenuation

Wave attenuation describes the reduction in wave height and energy as waves travel through a coastal environment. In mangrove forests, this occurs through interactions between moving water and vegetation. The roots, trunks and branches hinder water movement, creating hydrodynamic drag. This resistance transfers energy from the moving water into turbulence and other forms of energy dissipation. As a result, less wave energy reaches the landward edge of the forest. Field research by Horstman et al. (2014) investigated wave attenuation across mangrove areas with different vegetation densities. Their findings demonstrated that wave attenuation rates increased with the volumes of vegetation density. This suggests that the amount of vegetation within a given space can influence the effectiveness of mangroves as natural wave barriers. Forest width is another important factor. A wider forest provides a greater distance over which wave energy can be dissipated. Van Wesenbeeck et al. (2025) investigated this relationship through extensive numerical modelling. Their findings indicated that mangrove forests wider than 500 metres could dissipate approximately 75% or more of incoming wave energy under the modelled conditions. However, the effectiveness of narrower forests was more dependent on water levels, wave characteristics and vegetation density. These findings demonstrate that mangrove performance cannot be assessed using vegetation alone. The surrounding coastal conditions must also be considered.
